There is a new pattern I’ve been noticing across different fields since the rise of AI: a sudden re-evaluation of what each discipline is actually for. It is interesting, and honestly, a little funny.

Mathematicians, for instance, are now firmly declaring that their goal was never to uncover the truths of the universe. They weren't trying to figure out whether the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) is true or false. No, they are here to theorize the world, to organize our understanding of it, rather than to discover it.

Okay, fine. I’ll buy that. Just don't ask me tomorrow to accept the same thing from pharmacologists, claiming their goal was never to discover medicine, only to "understand" it. That they were never really looking for a cure for cancer or HIV; they were just striving to comprehend the drugs.

Anyway, these re-evaluations are fine. They are even noble and important. But I’ve run into a bit of a problem. My problem isn't the philosophy of science. It’s moral philosophy.

I always thought I was a good person. Maybe not the greatest researcher, sure, but a good person, someone who never felt bitter or envious of anyone else’s scientific breakthroughs.

That was until AI solved over five hundred math problems overnight.

Now, I’m starting to question my own goodness. I know I should have been thrilled. Instead, I felt crushed. And in that feeling, I’m starting to see myself a little more clearly: maybe I’m not quite the good person I thought I was.

I used to believe that doing research was about pushing the boundaries of human knowledge, untangling the mysteries of the universe, and improving the quality of life. Apparently, I was wrong. It turns out I was a researcher just to show off. To show off that I could navigate the unknown, wrestle with open problems, and not flinch. To show off how well I could code, write, prove, and analyze. To flaunt my intellectual superiority. Because if that weren't true, why would AI’s achievements hurt so much?

Some people say, "So what if AI can prove a few lemmas and theorems now? So what if it can write the codebase for a massive project in two hours? It’s no big deal. Now you’re free to think about bigger things, tackle harder problems, and be bolder in your ambitions."

But you know what? I took pride in proving that small lemma. I took pride in architecting a 30,000-line system from scratch. I prided myself on all of those skills. And now that something has come along that can do all of it effortlessly, it’s hard to swallow.

This hits much harder in academia than anywhere else, and here is why. Imagine a chef with a secret recipe that makes their restaurant more famous, and more profitable, than any other. Now imagine a robot is invented that hands out the best possible recipes to everyone. Suddenly, all restaurants are serving the exact same food, or at least something close enough that the line between them disappears. Is it natural for that chef to be upset? Of course it is. Their livelihood is on the line.

Academia isn't like that. Most of us here are underpaid anyway; it was never about the money. It’s about something else. And I’ve finally reached the point where I’m asking myself: what is that "something else"?

If it were truly about uncovering the unknown, I should be celebrating. Our pace of discovery has just accelerated infinitely. So, it wasn't that.

It was just pride. Pride in our own capabilities.

Which brings me to the ultimate question: I am clearly misaligned with the progress of humanity. So, am I actually a good person?
